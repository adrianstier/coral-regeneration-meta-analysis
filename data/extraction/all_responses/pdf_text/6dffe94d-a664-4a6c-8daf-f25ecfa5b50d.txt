     The effect of damage on the growth, reproduction and
      storage of lipids in the scleractinian coral Pocillopora
                       damicornis (Linnaeus)

                                                Selina Ward
      Centre for Coastal Management, Resource Science and Management. Southern Cross University,
                               Lismore 2480, New South Wales, Australia

       Received   17 November     1993; revision received   9 August   1994; accepted   4 November   1994




Abstract

A population of the scleractinian coral Pocilloporadamicornis (Linnaeus) was examined at Rottnest
Island, off the Western Australian coast. This is a temperate zone and an area under consid-
erable tourist pressure. Experiments were conducted at Little Salmon Bay and Mary Cove to
investigate the effect of mechanical damage on the growth, reproduction       and storage of lipid to
see whether patterns of energy allocation were affected. These parameters            were measured
simultaneously    14 times over 13 months.
   Damage had an adverse effect on the performance          of the corals. Dislodged corals had in-
creased mortality, decreased growth, decreased reproduction but no change in lipids. Fragmented
corals had no significant change in mortality, lipids or growth rate but had increased reproduc-
tion. The overall allocation of energy to growth, reproduction and lipid storage of dislodged corals
appeared to decrease whereas the overall output of the fragmented corals has increased.

Kevwords: Coral damage;         Growth   lipids; Pocillopora reproduction




1. Introduction

   Pocillopora damicornis (Linnaeus)       forms a small coral reef and occurs on many
limestone reefs on Rottnest Island, off the Western Australian coast. This area is close
to the southern-most      limit for this coral, where natural stress is generally greater
(temperature fluctuations,     storms etc.). Thus P. damicomis on Rottnest Island may be
more susceptible to damage caused by human impacts on the island such as snorkelling
and boating.

    Growth has been used many times as a measure of the performance                      of corals
 (Buddemeier      & Kinzie III, 1976). Growth patterns vary a great deal within species
 according to a host of environmental         factors. Daily growth ridges in the epithecae
 (Barnes, 1972) and high and low density growth bands develop according to the
favourability    of conditions.   Thus, the linear extension of the skeleton can be used as
 a measure of growth for the coral head. At times there is not a simple relationship
between size and age for corals as partial mortality, colony fission and colony fusion
may distort measures of growth (Hughes & Jackson, 1980). In this study, care was
taken to not sample damaged branches.
    Interspecific growth rates may vary a great deal, according to a host of environmental
factors, and have been used to indicate the health and status of corals (Buddemeier &
 Kinzie III, 1976; Brown and Scoffin, 1986). The linear extension rate of the coral
 skeleton may be measured, using the Alizarin staining technique (Lamberts, 1974), to
assess the response of coral to different environmental           conditions   (Gladfelter et al.,
 1978) and pollutants (Dodge et al., 1984).
    Corals contain relatively large quantities of lipid in their tissues (30-40x)          (Patton
et al., 1977; Stimson, 1987). The lipids may be either structural or storage (Patton et al.,
 1977). They are distributed throughout coral tissues and associated with reproductive
cells and eggs (Stimson, 1987). Small amounts are also present in mucus produced by
some corals (Meikle et al., 1988). Changes in the amount of lipid present in coral tis-
sues may be an indicator of stress. The quantity of lipid decreased in corals during the
El Nino warming event of 1983 (Glynn et al., 1985) and may decrease with decreas-
ing it-radiance (Stimson, 1987).
   The sexual reproductive       status of corals can be determined histologically (Kojis &
Quinn, 198 la,b) or by the collection of released gametes and planulae (Stimson, 1978;
Harriot, 1983; Richmond & Hunter, 1990; Wyers et al., 1991). Gametogenesis                 may be
affected by stress such as prolonged bleaching (Szmant & Gassman,               1990) and sexual
processes may be halted or reduced (Brown & Howard, 1985). Decreased reproduc-
tive activity may include failure to produce gametes or planulae, diminished gamete and
planulae production,       and a decreased amount of lipid per planula.
   This study aimed to investigate allocation of energy in corals at Rottnest Island.
Corals can use energy for three main functions: in growth, reproduction              and storage
of lipid (Sibly & Calow, 1986). Allocation of input to these three functions was mea-
sured simultaneously      in a population of corals over a period of 13 months. Growth was
measured as linear extension, storage of lipid was estimated gravimetrically                as the
percentage of tissue made up of lipid and reproductive            activity was investigated      by
measuring ova production, production of planulae and the lipid content of those planu-
lae. Stages of gametogenesis        were also monitored.
   To elucidate the effects of stress on allocation of coral energy, experiments using
mechanical damage were conducted at two sites. Mechanical                 damage is a prevalent
stress to corals at Rottnest Island and this naturally occurring damage was previously
monitored to determine the most common types of damage (Ward, 1991). The most
common types of damage were fragmentation              and dislodgement,     so these were used
as treatments for the damage experiments and the growth, reproduction                and storage
of lipid was measured for these corals. This allowed comparison between the patterns

of allocation for the damaged and the undamaged                     corals as well as between       individual
factors such as growth rates.



2. Methods

2.1.   Sites

   Rottnest Island lies 20 km off the coast of Western Australia at 32” S. Two sites were
sampled: Little Salmon Bay and Mary Cove. These sites are both on the west side of
the island and recieve some direct wave action. Corals grow on limestone but there is
no coral reef structure at these sites.

2.2. Experimental           design

   Manipulative   experiments were set up at Mary Cove and Little Salmon Bay. All
P. damicornis colonies chosen for this study appeared healthy, were between 30 and
50 cm in diameter, and were not shaded. Two treatments were used: fragmentation   and
dislodgement.   Fragmentation  involved breaking some branches from the colony with
a hammer but leaving the colony in place. Approximately 50% of branches were broken
off throughout the colony rather than just one section of the colony. The remaining
colony was sampled. Dislodged corals were knocked from the reef onto the substra-
tum below which was either sand, rock or seagrass bed. Corals were placed face up
at the time of damage and at each sampling time.
   An orthogonal design was used for this experiment so all possible combinations   of
these two treatments were used. At both sites there were fragmented only, dislodged
only, fragmented and dislodged and not fragmented, not dislodged corals (Table 1).

2.3.   Sampling procedure

   Sampling of colonies involved breaking three branches, E 5 cm long from each coral
at each date. Branches from the lowest parts of the colonies were avoided. Samples
were used to monitor growth, reproduction   and lipid storage. The branch tips were used
for growth measurement    and the rest of each branch for lipids and evaluation of the

2.4.   Growth

   In order to measure rates of linear extension of branches, colonies of P. damicornis
at both sites were stained in situ using the vital stain sodium alizarin sulfonate. The
dye was incorporated     into the skeleton of the coral colouring it purple where carbon-
ate deposition occurred. Between July and November, plastic bags were placed over
the corals just after dawn and tied in place with long strips of Dayglo tape in order to
isolate them from the surrounding sea water. Alizarin was injected into the plastic bags
at a concentration   of 15 mg.l-‘. The bags were left in place until just before sunset nine
hours later.
   Three branches were taken from the colony and the tips were placed into fresh water.
After 2-3 days the branch tips were washed with a jet of tap water until all the tissue
had been removed. They were then cut on a trim saw with a diamond blade so that
there was a flat face on which to measure the linear extension from the stain mark. Once
the coral had dried, the growth was measured using callipers (to + 0.1 mm) under a
magnifying lamp.

2.5.   Lipids

   The remaining branch sections from the above growth samples were placed in 10%
formaldehyde    in seawater for 24 h. The samples were then immersed in Bouin’s fixa-
tive for 2 wk to decalcify the skeleton. The tissue samples were placed in embedding
cassettes and dried in an oven for 24 h at 55 “C. The samples were then weighed (to
 & 0.0001 g) and immersed on a 2: 1 chloroform-methanol     solution for 24 h to dissolve
the lipids (Barnes & Blackstock, 1973; Stimson, 1987). The tissue samples were then
redried for 3 h and reweighed. The total lipid content was calculated by subtraction and
expressed as a percentage of the total tissue biomass.

2.6. Larval capture

   To estimate the number of larvae produced, portions of colonies were taken from
each coral 4 days before the new moon in March and transported       to the laboratory.
Colonies were placed in fresh sea water and larvae were collected, counted and stored
in a solution of 10% formaldehyde     in sea water.
   The number of planulae shed per piece of coral was standardised   by calculating the
relative surface area of each branch. Branches were weighed, placed in a Miles Tissue
Tek consul of paralhn wax at 60 ‘C for 5 s and then reweighed when cooled. The weight
of wax was then calculated and the number of planulae shed was divided by the weight
of wax.
   Captured larvae were analysed for lipid content following the methods used by
Barnes & Blackstock (1973) and Stimson (1987). Larvae from individual colonies were
                          S. Ward/J.   Exp. Mar. Biol. Ecol. 187 (1995) 193-206        191

placed in small vials and dried in an oven at 60 “C for 24 h. They were then covered
with a 2:l solution of chloroform methanol for 24 h to extract the lipid before being
dried again for 3 h and reweighed.

2.7.   Gametogenesis

   The number and position of samples taken for gametogenesis        was the same as that
of the lipid samples except that extra samples were taken 4 days before and 1 day after
the new moon in March. The samples were prepared for histology by placing them in
a solution of 10% formaldehyde in sea water for 24 h before being decalcified in Bouins
solution for ~2 wk. Tissue was then peeled off and placed into embedding cassettes.
Samples were processed through a graded series of alcohols and then cleared in 100%
chloroform. They were then impregnated      in paraplast wax and embedded in paraffin
wax. Sections of 6 pm were cut and these were stained with Gills Haematoxylin          and
Eosin.
   On each slide all polyps (50-100) were scored for their stage of gametogenesis      and
their planulae were counted. Stages were identified basically following those of Stoddart
(1984a,b). There were four stages of spermatogenesis      and two stages of oogenesis.
   Reproduction    includes both production   of planulae and gametes, so a measure of
reproductive output which included both components       was devised. An index of repro-
ductive potential (IRP) was calculated from the percentage of polyps with ova and
planulae for each colony at each date. The largest sum of the percentage of polyps
containing the two oogenic stages and/or planulae at each time point of each coral was
termed the IRP.

2.8.   Statistical analysis

   Comparison of growth, IRP and the percentage of lipid in tissue at different sites and
with different damage treatments was made using analysis of variance. Homogeneity
of variances was tested using Cochran’s test and, where necessary, appropriate trans-
formations were performed. The damage experiments were treated as an orthogonal
analysis of variance and, as site was included, this was a three-factor design.



3. Results

3.1. Mortality

   The most dramatic effect of damaging the corals was on their survival. There was
a very low mortality of undamaged     colonies at both sites with only one of the 14 un-
damaged colonies dying. There was a higher mortality amongst the dislodged and
fragmented   colonies with half of these corals dead by the end of the study period
(Table 2). Significantly more dislodged corals died than not dislodged corals and, al-
though more fragmented corals died than not fragmented corals, this difference was not
significant.

3.2. Growth

   Dislodged corals grew slower (between December 1988 and June 1989) than those
which were not dislodged (Table 3) (Fig. 1) but there was no significant effect of
fragmentation   on the amount of cumulative growth. Corals at Little Salmon Bay grew
faster than those at Mary Cove (Table 3).

3.3. Lipids

   There was no clear pattern of the effect of dislodgement, fragmentation   or site on the
quantity of lipid in tissue of experimental corals at Little Salmon Bay and Mary Cove
on the 12 dates the analyses of lipids were conducted. In November the dislodged corals
had less lipid in the tissues than the undislodged      colonies (Fig. 2) and on the 7th
February colonies from Mary Cove had a higher quantity of lipid than those from Little
Salmon Bay.

3.4. Larval capture

    There were numerically fewer larvae captured in dislodged than not dislodged cor-
 als, more in fragmented than not fragmented corals and fewer at Mary Cove than at
 Little Salmon Bay but none of these differences were statistically significant (Fig. 3).
    The lipid content of the larvae was most frequently 50-60%. Some corals produced
very few larvae and these were difficult to analyse. Although there was less lipid in the
larvae captured from corals which were dislodged than those which were not dislodged
 and more in not fragmented than fragmented corals, the differences were not signifi-
cant (Fig. 4). The data for larval capture were significantly correlated with the num-
ber of planulae from the histological      data, although the relationship      is not strong
(r = 0.56, p< 0.05, n = 46).
    The index of reproductive potential (IRP) was significantly larger in fragmented than
unfragmented     corals. In dislodged corals the IRP was smaller than that of the corals
which were not dislodged but not significantly so. There was no significant effect of site
(Table 4). These trends are the same as those found for the data on capture of larvae
shed in the laboratory (Fig. 4).
    Corals with the four damage treatments:        fragmentation   only, dislodgement    only,
dislodgement    and fragmentation    and no damage followed similar gametogenesis         pat-
terns. All groups showed the disappearance       of late sperm and ova on 7th February and
5th April and all had major planulation         in March (Ward, 1992). All four groups
planulated in February as well as March although the February event was consider-
ably smaller.

4 . Di s c u s s i o n

   Damaging the corals clearly affected their performance. Firstly, dislodgement caused
high mortality with the majority of dislodged corals dying by the end of the study period.
Dislodged corals also had slower growth. A dislodged coral which lays in sand or on
sea grass may be exposed to increased sedimentation      and less light. Without its usual
attachment, the coral is more susceptible to damage through water movement as it may
be carried across the substratum,   causing increased abrasion and sections of the col-
ony to face into the sand. Colonies may be battered against rocks or thrown into nearby
sea grass beds. Dislodged corals in this study sometimes died very quickly following
a storm, perhaps as a result of shut-down reaction occurring (Antonius,       1981, 1988).
   Mortality was high in the fragmented corals. Fragmentation       had no obvious effect
on the cumulative growth of colonies, which is contrary to findings of Simpson (1987)
who reported that growth of colonies damaged by a tropical cyclone was slower after

colonies in this study were monitored and showed a trend of low growth but these either
died or disappeared rapidly. Bak & Criens (1981) conducted a fragmentation          experi-
ment and found an initial decrease in living coral cover followed by overgrowth by living
coral. Towards the end of the experiment, growth was less than the control quadrant
but mortality was very low until an outbreak of a disease killed all the fragments. During
this study, there was increased mortality of fragmented corals following one large storm
near the end of 1988. Before this storm, mortality of fragmented corals was very low.
Fragmentation     may make colonies more susceptible to sudden events such as storms
and disease outbreaks.
   With respect to growth and mortality, dislodgement appears to be a more severe type
of damage than fragmentation,    as it caused a decrease in cumulative growth as well as
an increase in mortality.

    Corals at Rottnest Island had a high lipid content. The 29-46x              of tissue weight
made up by lipid recorded here is slightly higher than past studies with Patton et al.
(1977) finding 34% for P. capitata and Stimson (1987) finding 30-40x                 for P. dami-
comis. It is possible that the high percentage        of lipid found in this study may be a
characteristic   of corals in cooler water. However, there are no suitable studies with
which to compare these results.
    There were fluctuations in the amount of lipid present in the tissue of the sampled
corals throughout the year. Corals had more lipids in their tissues between August and
February than between March and the following August. On a finer scale the quantity
of lipid decreased at the February, March and April new moons and had increased by
the sampling period 2 wk after these new moons. These reductions coincide with the
loss of planulae and gametes over these periods. Damage did not obviously affect the
quantity of lipids in the coral tissue as there was no difference between treatments in
the quantity of lipid present on any date, or either pre- or post-planulation.
    The reproductive    activity of P. damicomis at Rottnest Island was affected by both
dislodgement    and fragmentation.     The IRP was lower for dislodged corals due to re-
duced numbers of both ova and planulae. Dislodged corals are frequently exposed to
greater sediment stress, due to resuspension,        because they are on the substrate sur-
rounded by sand. This may cause a decrease in light available to the corals which is
often compounded       by a slight decrease in light due to increased depth as the corals
usually fall from the reef downwards.
    An increase in the IRP resulted from fragmenting the colonies. Fragmention             during
this study involved removing branches throughout the colony so this may have caused
an increase in light available to many of the remaining branches. Kojis & Quinn (1984)
found that fecundity in Acropora palijha varied proportionately            with high levels and
correlated with water clarity and sedimentation.       The most fecund section of the branch
is the mid section below the growing tips and in an intact colony these sections are often
in relative shade. Rinkevich (1989) used labelled carbon to conclude that the energy
requirements    for planulae production     in hermatypic corals are supported by trans-
location of products from algal photosynthesis.       Fragmentation    may have increased the
light available for the fecund areas and this may have caused the increase in repro-
ductive potential.
   PociZlopora damicornis at Rottnest Island follows the pattern of this and other coral
species at other temperate zone and subtropical reefs in that growth is fastest during
the warmest months, in this case February, March and April (Stoddart, 1984a; Ward
 1991). This is in contrast to tropical reefs where growth is usually fastest during the
winter months (Highsmith,        1979). The period of increased growth at Rottnest Island
is short with only 3 months growth which is markedly faster than any other time.
   No comparable data on recovery rates for fragmentation         and dislodgement in tropical
areas, using the parameters measured in this study could be found, but it seems likely
that recovery would be faster in the tropics where growth is faster. Kay & Liddle (1989)
studied the effects of trampling by tourists on areas of coral reef off Heron Island on
the Great Barrier Reef and this involved considerable fragmentation            of colonies. They
found different levels of destruction on different sections of the reef, but did not look
closely at recovery rates so a comparison       is difficult. In the relatively warm water of

 Costa Rica, Guzman & Cortes (1989) found that Porites lob&a used fragmentation               as
 a dispersal mechanism and exhibited rapid injury recovery and high resistance to en-
vironmental    stress.
    The results of damage experiments involving dislodgement          and fragmentation    gen-
erally reflected the results of the monitoring      of ‘naturally’ occurring damage on the
island (Ward, 1991). Generally, corals which were found dislodged died or did not
 appear to grow noticeably. Often, they would have abraded sections where the coral
had faced the substratum and this area would become overgrown with algae. In con-
trast, colonies which were found fragmented either died or appeared to have a full
recovery.
    Gray (1989) suggested that the application of a stressor may cause an alarm reac-
tion followed by compensation        and a return to normal and that, if the application
continues, the organism may die. He hypothesizes that initially an organism will divert
energy from growth into reproduction        but on further application     of the stressor the
fecundity will decrease. Thompson (1983, 1984) found that sea urchins on a depleted
food ration decreased their gametic output but, when considered with their accompa-
nying decrease in growth, this represented       an increase in reproductive     effort. As the
mortality figures suggest, the dislodged corals were dying or at least not performing well.
These results suggest that they may have shifted allocation away from growth to
reproduction.    Although the IRP was reduced in comparison with corals which were not
dislodged, this may not reflect the reproductive       effort of the corals as the proportion
of total energy used for reproduction     may have increased,
    The IRP of the fragmented corals was increased due to more brooded larvae rather
than large numbers of ova. The increase in reproductive potential does not appear to
be a trade-off reaction to stress as neither the growth nor the content of lipid simul-
taneously decreased.
    As discussed in Ward (1991) there are two forms of allocation operating in corals
at Rottnest Island. Corals, which produced brooded larvae produced ova, showed a
decrease in growth, an increase in reproductive        lipid and a decrease in storage lipid.
Corals which did not produce brooded larvae did not produce ova, showed more rapid
growth, had less reproductive      lipid and more storage lipid. Now the allocation of
damaged corals can be compared with the “normal pattern”.
   Although the seasonal allocation patterns were basically the same for damaged
corals, some differences were apparent.        For dislodged corals almost all produced
brooded larvae and there was, overall, a reduced output. Growth was decreased and
there was a reduction in reproductive      potential and no change in lipids. In contrast,
the fragmented colonies appeared to increase their output. There was no significant
change in cumulative growth but reproductive         potential was increased and there was
no change in lipids.

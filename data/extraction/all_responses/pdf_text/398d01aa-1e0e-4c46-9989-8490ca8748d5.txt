            PARTIAL MORTALITY IN THREE SPECIES OF
         REEF-BUILDING CORALS AND THE RELATION WITH
                      COLONY MORPHOLOGY

                         Erik H. Meesters, lneke Wesseling and
                                    Rolf P. M. Bak
                                            ABSTRACT
       Partial tissue mortality (lesions) in three coral species with distinctly different colony mor-
    phologies was quantified in a series of field surveys on a shallow reef. Extent and type of
    partial mortality was related to differences in size and morphology of the colonies within and
    between species. Lesion size-frequency distributions were found to be very skewed to the
    right, meaning that most partial mortality is small in size and well within the regeneration
    capabilities of all coral species. However, large lesions may make a considerable contribution
    in terms of surface area to total partial mortality. Within partial mortality two types of lesions
    must be distinguished. Type I lesions are completely surrounded by living tissue and their
    occurrence is mostly related to non-bottom associated processes. Type II lesions, at the edge
    of a colony, are only partly surrounded by living tissue, and are caused mostly by bottom
    associated processes. The ratio of number of type Wtype I lesions differed between species
    and was related to the ratio of colony edge (circumference)/colony surface area in the three
    species studied. Type II lesions were larger in size than type I lesions and can make a large
    contribution to total partial mortality. Species differed in the number of type I and type II
    lesions per colony and per unit of tissue area. Type II lesions were almost absent on branched
    colonies, but very frequent on colonies of massive species. Lesion number increased with
    colony size in the massive species. Most partial mortality is caused by bottom related pro-
    cesses and our results show that "escape in height" is a significant feature in the life history
    strategies of corals. The surveys showed smaJl colonies to be very vulnerable to partial
    mortality. Because their circumference/surface area ratio is high, they are very susceptible to
    colony edge (i.e., bottom-associated) processes that cause mortality. Consequently, small
    colonies will often suffer whole colony death. On the other hand, large colonies, although
    unlikely to escape partial mortality, will less often suffer complete mortality. The relationship
    between this ratio and susceptibility to partial mortality holds as well within species as
    between species and suggests an important effect of colony morphology on survival. Colony
    genetic identity also affected susceptibility to partial mortality. Other factors that may influ-
    ence sensitivity to mortality are discussed. Regeneration capabilities of corals suggest that
    scleractinian corals have become adapted to the very common occurrence of small lesions.



   Damage to the living surfaces of corals is caused by abiotic and biotic distur-
bances, such as hurricanes (Knowlton et aI., 1981; Rogers et aI., 1991), diseases
(Peters, 1984), emersion at low tide (Fishelson, 1973) predation (Glynn, 1988;
Bak and van Eys, 1975; Robertson, 1970) and other interspecific interactions
including interactions with humans (Lang, 1971; Pearson, 1981; Bak et aI., 1982;
Hudson and Diaz, 1988).
   Corals and many other taxa are modular colonial organisms and consequently
the modules or subunits can function and survive as physiologically independent
entities. A group of modules (colony) may be divided into many separate parts
through processes such as growth and tissue shrinkage, fission, and fusion
(Hughes and Jackson, 1980; Bak et aI., 1981; Jackson and Coates, 1986). Partial
mortality, the ability of corals to survive the loss of part of the living tissue, is
central in their life history strategies. The genetic individual may survive in bits
and pieces. Demographic studies of colonial reef organisms taking partial mor-
tality into account suggest that coral colonies loose more tissue through partial

mortality than through whole colony death (Hughes, 1984; Hughes and Jackson,
1985; Done, 1987; Babcock, 1991; Lasker, 1991; McFadden, 1991).
   When a colony surface is damaged the tissue around this lesion grows inwards
to recover the wound (Fishelson, 1973; Loya, 1976; Bak et aI., 1977; Bak and
Steward-van Es, 1980; Bak and Criens, 1981; Bak, 1983). If this fails, the result
is partial mortality that remains visible as a bare patch of skeleton on the surface
of the coral colony.
   Regeneration of lesions is influenced by lesion size, interspecific interactions,
and environmental conditions. Research has shown that different species are char-
acterized by different regeneration rates, associated with their life history strate-
gies (Bak et aI., 1977; Bak and Steward-van Es, 1980; Bak and Criens, 1981;
Bak, 1983; Meesters et aI., 1992; Meesters and Bak, 1993). If lesions are above
a certain size they will not be completely regenerated (Bak and Steward-van Es,
1980) and can be colonized by algae and excavating sponges that may overgrow
the coral tissue or weaken the skeleton, respectively.
   Obviously, regeneration rates, the occurrence of coral colony surface damage
and the role of partial mortality are key elements in the ecology of stony corals.
There are no data, however, on the role of these processes on the reef, on possible
differences between species and variation between colony growth forms.
   The linkage between growth-form/mortality processes is not often considen::d
by reef ecologists, except for factors such as wave action and turbulence (Woodley
et aI., 1981). Jackson (1979) reviewed morphological strategies in colonial or-
ganisms and suggested that the ratio of proximal circumference (colony edge) to
colony tissue surface area is an important characteristic in the relative vulnera-
bility of species to bottom-associated processes.
   In this study we assess partial mortality (colony surface lesions) on a shallow
reef for three species, a branching coral species (Acropora palmata) and two
massive species (Porites astreoides and Diploria strigosa). We address the fOiI-
lowing questions: 1) What are the characteristics of partial mortality for each
species? 2) Are lesion characteristics indicative for different causative mecha-
nisms? 3) How do the characteristics of partial mortality relate to different life
history strategies?


                                    MATERIALS      AND METHODS

Survey and Species.-Partial mortality was assessed on 446 colonies of 3 abundant coral species. The
corals were surveyed at three sites along the coast of the Caribbean island of Cural<ao,Netherlands
Antilles. The sites were located, in front of the Avila beach hotel, at Carmabi buoy 2, and in front of
Seaquarium. At each site colonies were surveyed in 2- or 4-m wide transects depending on density
of coral colonies. Transects were parallel to the shore in the shallow reef coral community (Depth
1.5-3.5 m) of the Acropora palmata zone and the barren zone (Bak, 1977). We surveyed the most
abundant species, notably A. palmata (Lamarck, 1816), Diploria strigosa (Dana, 1848), and Porites
astreoides Lamarck, 1816. In each species, colony and lesion characteristics of the first 50 colonies
encountered were measured. Due to low densities fewer colonies were surveyed occasionally.
   The three species differ in colony morphology and life history traits. Acropora palmata, a branching
coral, can occur in dense stands that efficiently dissipate the high water energy characteristic of their
exposed habitat. Most colonies are well within the breaker zone where they may fragment during
storms (Highsmith et aI., 1980). Sexual recruits of A. palmata were virtually absent on these reefs
(Bak and Engel, 1979), and asexual reproduction by fragmentation may constitute the main mode of
reproduction (Highsmith, 1982). The two other species also occur in the A. palmata zone, but are not
exclusive to it, their distribution ranging from 0.5 to 30 m (Bak, 1977). The hemispherical colonies
of Diploria strigosa, a brain coral, occur in sizes up to 1 m in diameter. Porites astreoides colonies
are a flat to hemi-spherical, bumpy or smooth polypary surface, not wider than 40 em. The three
species are hermaphroditic (Szmant, 1986). A. palmata and D. strigosa are broadcast spawners that
have one gametogenesis each year and produce few recruits (Bale and Engel, 1979). P. astreoides has
840                         BULLETIN OF MARINE SCIENCE, VOL. 58, NO.3,   1996


a high recruitment success (Bak and Engel, 1979) and releases brooded larvae on an almost monthly
basis in Jamaica (Chomesky and Peters, 1987).
Colonies.-A colony was defined as an autonomous mass of skeleton with living tissue. This includes
colonies separated by partial mortality into disconnected patches of living tissue but morphologically
still one entity. Two patches of living tissue on one colony were recorded as one fission event, three
patches as two fission events, etc. The bare skeleton in between was measured and recorded as a
lesion. The total surface area of each colony was estimated by regarding it as a circle, a rectangle, a
hemisphere, or a combination of a hemisphere and a cylinder, and applying the appropriate geometrical
formula. Colony diameter was estimated by taking the square root of the product of the maximal
length and perpendicular width.
   In Acropora palmata three random branches were measured on each colony. Consequently, we only
estimated branch surface area. We distinguished upper and undersides of branches. In the two massive
species there was no underside. Interspecific comparisons were made without taking branch undersides
into account.
Lesion Type and Lesion Size.-A pilot study showed two main types of lesions to exist: a) Type I
lesions. These are lesions on all sides surrounded by living tissue; b) Type II lesions. These lesions
are only partly surrounded by living tissue. Both type of lesions can be found on the same colony,
   Lesion size in the pilot study was found to be approximately log normally distributed. Based on
these results and for practical reasons we assigned lesions to one of six size classes: ±0.1-1 cm2, 1-
4, 4-16, 16-49, 49-100, and larger than 100 em'. Lesions larger than 100 cm2 were measured to the
nearest 50 cm2• To calculate the total lesion area of each colony we used geometric means of the
lesion size classes except for lesions larger than 100 cm2 where the log of the actual size was taken.

frequency distributions of Figure 1. Very obvious is the difference between type
I and type II lesions. Distributions of type I lesions, lesions completely surrounded

size class dominate the distribution. The distributions of type II lesions, lesions
that are only partially surrounded by living tissue, are more evenly spread over
the lesion size classes, indicating more lesions in the larger size classes and a
higher mean size.
   The lesion size-frequency distributions of the three species (Fig. 1) differed
depending on species, lesion size and lesion type (test for 3-factor interaction
using log-linear model of species, lesion size class, and lesion type; G = 37.1,

lumped). Type II lesions differed between the two non-branching species (G =
19.9, df = 5, P < 0.005). Diploria had fewer lesions in the smallest and more
in the largest size class. Type II lesion numbers in Acropora were too low for
statistical comparisons.
   Type I and type II lesions were not equally distributed over the three species
(G = 338.8, df = 2, P < 0.00001). Type II lesions in Acropora represented only
7.5% of all lesions counted, while they represented 51 and 69% in Diploria and
Porites, respectively (Fig. 1).

size class. Partial mortality was much more common on the upperside of branches.

Fission.- The number of colonies that actually showed fission, the separation into
disconnected parts of living tissue by partial mortality, was low. In Acropora

these cases fission produced an average of 3.6 colonies per parental colony. In

in Porites produced 2.6 colonies per parent colony.

Inter-colony  Differences.-GENETlC EFFECTS. The sampling design used to measure
the branching coral can be used to test whether there are differences between
colonies in vulnerability. On the average 49.2% of all sampled branches had at
least one lesion (Table 7). This proportion did not differ between the three sam-
pling sites (P = 0.5, G-test). To determine if colonies differ in their vulnerability
to partial mortality we tested whether lesions were randomly distributed over
colonies. The expected proportion of branches showing partial mortality when
only one branch is sampled of each colony can be calculated as the mean pro-
portion of branches with lesions of all the first, all the second, and all the third
branches that were sampled on each colony. With this proportion one can calculate
the expected percentage when sampling three branches from the same colony if
these are independent of colony identity. This proportion can then be compared
with the observed proportion.
   When sampling three branches from one colony, the chance that at least one
of them is damaged will be 1 - P (all three without a lesion). For example, for
site A this would be 1 - 0.443 = 0.92 [56% with one or more lesions, gives (1
- 0.56) = 0.44 with no lesion]. Because A. palmata colonies often have many
branches, it should be almost impossible for a colony not to have at least one
branch with partial mortality.
   The expected frequencies and results of the comparison between observed and
expected frequencies (Table 7) show that significantly fewer colonies had a lesion
than expected when branches were independent of colony identity (paired t-test,
t = 16.2, df = 2, P = 0.004). Test for non-randomness, the runs test (Sokal and
Rohlf 1981), indicated that lesions were aggregated within colonies (P = 0.02,
0.003, and 0.0001 for sites A, B, and C, respectively). Thus, colonies of Acropora
palmata differ in their vulnerability to damage.

differences are related to morphological characteristics of the species studied.
Type I versus Type II Lesions.-A remarkable phenomenon in partial mortality
is the occurrence and significant differences between two types of lesions: one,
surrounded on all sides by tissue (type I), and one which is only partly surrounded
by living tissue (type II). Most type I lesions are very small, and are always
smaller than type II lesions. As a consequence, a type II lesion will have a stronger
impact on a colony than a type I lesion, because the colony will have less tissue
to support growth, reproduction, and regeneration. Thus type II lesions are po-
tentially more detrimental to a colony's long-term survival. However, lesion fre-
quencies were highly dependent on species, colony size and lesion type.
   The differences between lesion types and species can be explained by differ-
ences in the causes of the two lesion types and the effect of colony morphology.
   The two types of lesions are probably indicative of different mortality causing
processes. Type II lesions are always on the edge of the living tissue (see Materials
and Methods) and therefore they occur mostly close to the bottom. Type I lesions
are completely surrounded by living tissue and occur over the whole colony, but
not on the edge of the living tissue. This means that type II lesions are more
likely to originate from agents that are restricted to the bottom, while type I lesions
originate more from agents that move freely in the water column. For example,
interspecific interactions with corals, sea urchins, and polychaetes, scouring sand,
and moving coral fragments are more likely to cause type II lesions. Urchins and
polychaetes will start at the edge of the living tissue. Sand and moving fragments
are, solely because of gravity, also more likely to cause damage close to the
bottom. Fish, divers, and diseases, are likely to cause more lesions of type I.
Colony Morphology.-If we assume that our hypothesis about the origin of the
different lesion types is true, Acropora will have little chance of type II mortality
and most partial mortality should consist of type I lesions. Porites has relative to
its colony surface area the largest circumference and should have the highest
number of type II lesions relative to the number of type I lesions. Diploria should
be intermediate Porites and Acropora.
   We compared the ratio type II/type I lesions with the ratio of proximal colony
circumference/colony surface area for the three species (Fig. 5). The type II/type
I ratio not only indicates the relative importance of peripheral versus non-periph-
eral processes, but to some extent also the total mortality, since type II lesions
are larger than type I lesions.
   The results (Fig. 5) show that colony morphology is closely related to the
specific susceptibility to partial mortality. Branching corals, such as Acropora,
having a very small proximal-perimeter/surface-area ratio are relatively safe from
type II lesions because they have relatively little colony surface close to the
bottom. However, because of the small attachment area the risk of breakage is
high. In Acropora this risk is offset by an "escape in height" from bottom as-
sociated processes that cause mortality (Table 1, Fig. 5).
   The small attachment area may also explain why type I lesions in the branching
coral are larger than in the massive corals. Type I lesions may enlarge through
interspecific competition between coral polyps and organisms that settle in the
lesions. In massive species the lesion may eventually reach the edge of the tissue
and turn into a type II lesion. This is less likely to happen in the branching coral,
because of the small attachment area.
   Diploria strigosa colonies have much surface area relative to the colony cir-
cumference, although less than in the branching coral. Consequently, Diploria

   P. astreoides is more frequently damaged by type II lesions than the other
species. Relative to the colony's surface area it has a large perimeter, which ren-
ders it relatively more susceptible to damage from benthic organisms or abiotic
agents like sand scouring or moving coral fragments.
   The low incidence of fission can be explained by the effects of colony mor-
phology on partial mortality. The percentage of colonies originating from fission
was much lower than reported earlier (Hughes and Jackson 1985; Babcock 1991).
Babcock (1991) concluded that colony fission is likely to constitute a cost rather
than a benefit, because there is increased probability of further partial mortality
and total fecundity decreases. Colony fission in foliaceous species can be very
high (Hughes and Jackson 1985). Colonies of foliaceous species have twice as
much perimeter relative to colony surface area as hemispherical colonies of the
same diameter (assuming a circular morphology). Consequently, partial mortality
in foliaceous colonies will involve more type II lesions than in hemispherical
colonies. Because fission involves type II lesions, foliaceous species are more
likely to show fission.
Evo/utionairy Aspects.-Smalliesions      are more abundant than larger lesions and
most lesions were smaller than 1 cm2• This size is well within the regeneration
capabilities of corals (Bak et al. 1977; Bak and Steward-van Es 1980; Bak 1983;
Meesters et al. 1992; Meesters and Bak 1993). Bak and Steward-van Es (1980)
noted that regeneration stops after a certain time interval presumably because of
an intrinsic factor. Therefore, large lesions, too large to be recovered within the
regeneration time interval, will never be closed. The lesion size-frequency distri-
butions do not show the lesions that have been regenerated. Very many small
                   MEESTERS ET AL.: MORTALITY AND MORPHOLOGY IN CORALS           849


lesions must recover in a matter of days. The great capacity of corals to regenerate
small lesions must have evolved as a consequence of the relatively high frequency
with which these lesions are being inflicted. Of course, physiological limitations
in the energy translocation necessary for the regeneration are also involved and
should be investigated (Meesters et aI., 1994).
   Colony size influenced the susceptibility to mortality by both massive species.
Small colonies often showed no partial mortality, although per unit area they had
on average the highest partial mortality. This indicates that small colonies are
more likely to escape damage than larger colonies, but when damage occurs,
mortality may be total (Hughes and Jackson, 1980, 1985; Rylaarsdam, 1983;
Babcock, 1991). Because small colonies have much circumference relative to the
total surface area, they will be more susceptible to benthic agents that cause type
II lesions. Small colonies of Porites astreoides are generally flatter than small
colonies of Diploria strigosa and this may explain the relative dominance of type
II lesions on small P. astreoides colonies. Because type II lesions are larger than
type I lesions, P. astreoides colonies will also more often experience whole colony
mortality. The life history strategy of P. astreoides allows high juvenile mortality.
The species has the second highest rate of recruitment on our reefs, only preceded
by Agaricia agaricites (Bak and Engel, 1979).
   Clearly, the consequences of shape and size are not limited to colonies within
a species, but extend to the general colony morphology of any species. Species
that have mostly small or flat colonies have much circumference relative to sur-
face area and should be vulnerable to mortality by type II lesions. The vulnera-
bility of species with small maximum size colonies may have led to the devel-
opment of a set of reproductive traits that is commonly called brooding (Harrison
and Wallace, 1990).
   In many marine invertebrates brooding is associated with small adult size
(Strathmann and Strathmann, 1982). In corals brooding does not appear to be
associated with adult polyp size, but with maximum colony size (Kojis and Quinn,
1982; Van Moorsel, 1983; Szmant, 1986; Babcock, 1991; Soong, 1993). Species
with small maximum colony sizes generally brood and we hypothesize that this
mode of reproduction has evolved to overcome the high mortality that is asso-
ciated with this type of colony morphology.
   Another way to cope with a higher chance of mortality resulting from mor··
phology is to develop a high regeneration potential. This has been shown in
platelike colonies of the coral Meandrina meandrites (Linnaeus, 1758). This spe-
cies has the highest regeneration rate ever reported (Meesters and Bak, 1993) and
is very successful in interspecific interactions (Lang, 1973). Both characteristics
seem to indicate that this species is extraordinarily adapted to the possibility of
damage by interactions at its periphery. The absence of partial mortality observed
on colonies of the genus Mycetophyllia (pers. obs.) is possibly also an indication
of highly developed regeneration potential.

Other Factors Affecting Partial Mortality.-Partial mortality is not just related to
colony morphology. The results suggest that different genetic characteristics with-
in a species are also important. Branches with partial mortality were more likely
to be part of the same colony. This is analogous to the results of Babcock (1991)
for massive colonies who found that damaged colonies had more chance of future
mortality than undamaged colonies.
   The three species may also differ in their susceptibility to disease. The extreme
difference in the mean size of type II lesions in Acropora when compared with
the massive species may be caused by the specific susceptibility of the genus
850                         BULLETIN OF MARINE SCIENCE. VOL. 58. NO.3. 1996



Acropora to the white band disease (WBD; Bak and Criens, 1981; Gladfelter,
1982). Affected colonies have been recorded continuously ever since this disease
has first been reported in the late seventies.
   Species may also vary in their attractiveness to predators. Porites astreoides
appears to be attacked more often by the polychaete Hermodice carunculata than
Diploria strigosa (Ott and Lewis, 1972). Damage by fish bites (Scaridae) has also
been observed more often on P. astreoides (Meesters, unpubi. data). Acropora
palmata may be protected by the formation of dense stands and its occurrence in
a the normally exposed habitat, both which may limit the accessibility of the
colonies to damaging agents.             .
   Another important aspect in partial mortality is regeneration of damage. The
three species differ in regeneration potential. Acropora palmata has one of the
highest regeneration rates found in corals (Bak, 1983; Meesters et aI., 1992) and
fragments fuse rapidly (Bak and Criens, 1981, 1982). Consequently, fragments
have a high survivorship, which enhances asexual recruitment (Highsmith et aI.,
1980; Bak and Criens, 1981). Regeneration in Porites is generally faster than in
Diploria (Meesters et aI., 1992), possibly because of the thicker tissue layer in P.
astreoides. These differences in regeneration rates may also affect lesion size-
frequency distributions.

                                           CONCLUSIONS

   Our main conclusions are: 1) partial mortality in scleractinian corals is common
but mainly small in size and well within the regeneration capabilities of corals;
2) partial mortality is strongly related to size and shape of the colony. Colonies
escape in size (less mortality per unit colony area on bigger colonies) and in
height (less mortality in branching colonies); 3) The ratio proximal circumference/
total surface area of a colony is an indicator of the susceptibility of the colony
to tissue mortality. This is NIOZ publication no. 3054.

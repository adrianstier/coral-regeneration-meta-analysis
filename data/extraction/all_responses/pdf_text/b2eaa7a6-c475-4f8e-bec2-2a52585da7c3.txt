Abstract

   Regeneration of artificial lesions was studied along a depth gradient from 5–35 m in the
Caribbean corals Porites astreoides (brown morph) and Stephanocoenia michelinii (massive
morph). During the first 3–4 weeks of the regeneration process tissue recovery in both coral
species was higher in shallow water than in deep water colonies. After 7 weeks when the lesions
were almost closed, however, shallow as well as deep water colonies had restored the damage to
the same degree, showing that regeneration is a high priority process in coral ecology. The deep
water colonies did not show signs of photoacclimatization. The zooxanthellae densities and the
total chlorophyll concentration per algal cell in the coral tissue did not vary significantly with
depth. Furthermore, there was no decrease with depth of the chlorophyll a /c 2 ratio, which would
have indicated photoacclimatization by means of a relative increase of chlorophyll c 2 which is
more efficient in using the blue light available at depth. One possibility which may explain the
slower tissue recovery in deep water colonies during the first stage of the regeneration process, is a
combination of the reduced light levels at depth and a lack of chlorophyll acclimatization in the
deep water colonies.  1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Corals; Damage; Depth gradient; Lesions; Regeneration; Tissue recovery




1. Introduction

     Regeneration is an important life history strategy in corals (Bak, 1983; Meesters et al.,

1992; Meesters and Bak, 1995; Meesters et al., 1996). Corals can be damaged as a result
of various biotic and abiotic causes (see review by Pearson, 1981) and with failing of
regeneration, lesions will become a permanent feature on the colony surface (Meesters et
al., 1996, 1997b).
   Regeneration has been studied in relation to colony size (Loya, 1976), spatial
competition (Bak et al., 1977), lesion size and shape (Bak and Steward Van-Es, 1980;
Meesters et al., 1997a; Oren et al., 1997), lesion position on the colony (Meesters et al.,
1992; Meesters and Bak, 1995), environmental factors (Lester and Bak, 1985),
sedimentation (Meesters et al., 1992), coral bleaching (Meesters and Bak, 1993),
fecundity (Van Veghel and Bak, 1994), growth (Bak, 1983; Meesters et al., 1994), and as
an ecophysiological trait in coral morphs (Nagelkerken and Bak, 1998). However, all
these studies were largely limited to the reef crest, the reef terrace or the shallow reef
slope. Various coral species are distributed along a relatively large depth range, but little
is known of the regeneration of coral colonies in deeper water.
   Corals often have limited energy budgets, and energy is partitioned over important
processes such as growth, reproduction and regeneration (Bak, 1983; Edmunds and
Spencer Davies, 1986; Spencer Davies, 1991). Regeneration is fuelled by the polyps
surrounding and in the vicinity of the lesion and requires a considerable amount of
energy (Meesters et al., 1994, 1997a; Oren et al., 1997). In spite of the high energetic
cost, a trade-off in energy exists in favor of regeneration and to the detriment of growth
(Bak, 1983; Meesters et al., 1994) and fecundity (Van Veghel and Bak, 1994), which
indicates the importance of this process.
   Light is of primary importance for the survival of reef-building corals (Chalker et al.,
1988). Photosynthetically fixed carbon is translocated from the symbiotic algae to the
coral host and serves as a source of energy (Patton et al., 1977; Crossland et al., 1980;
Muscatine et al., 1984; Stimson, 1987). Light intensity decreases with depth (e.g.
Bosscher and Meesters, 1992) and corals living at greater depths may have less energy
for regeneration and other processes. Several studies have already shown that corals
growing in deeper waters and / or at reduced light levels show a decrease in other
energy-requiring physiological functions, such as growth rate (Dustan, 1975; Hubbard
and Scaturo, 1985; Huston, 1985; Bosscher and Meesters, 1992), fecundity (Kojis and
Quinn, 1984) and respiration rate (Spencer Davies, 1977, 1980; Chalker et al., 1983).
   Our hypothesis is that as a result of the decreasing light intensity at increasing depth
corals have less energy reserves available for the regeneration process, resulting in a
lower regeneration rate in corals growing at greater depths. To test this hypothesis, the
regeneration of artificial lesion was studied in two Caribbean coral species along a depth
range from 5–35 m. To account for the effect of photoacclimatization—the process as a
result of which corals can acclimatize to lower light levels trough, for example, a change
in the photosynthetic pigment content and organization—we measured densities of
zooxanthellae and concentrations of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll c 2 per algal cell.


2. Materials and methods

     The scleractinian corals Porites astreoides (Lamarck) and Stephanocoenia michelinii
                 I. Nagelkerken et al. / J. Exp. Mar. Biol. Ecol. 234 (1999) 29 – 39   31


(Milne-Edwards and Haime) were selected to study the regeneration of artificial lesions
in relation to water depth. Both coral species have a relatively flat colony surface with
small polyps, facilitating infliction of artificial lesions, and resulting in an evenly
distributed light regime over the colony’s surface.
   The study site was located at the Seaquarium reef on the leeward coast of Curaçao,
Netherlands Antilles. A detailed description of the reef structure and distribution of
corals on the reefs of Curaçao is given by Bak (1975), (1977).
   Along a depth-gradient from 5 to 35 m, 41 colonies of Porites astreoides and 29
colonies of Stephanocoenia michelinii were haphazardly selected. These two coral
species both consist of at least two morphs, which show a different rate of regeneration
(Nagelkerken and Bak, 1998). In this study we used the brown morph of P. astreoides
and the massive morph of S. michelinii (see Nagelkerken and Bak, 1998). Cylindrical
lesions of approximately 1 cm in diameter and 2–3 mm deep were inflicted in situ on
top of the experimental colonies, during January 1993. The lesions were made with a
small grinding stone attached to a pneumatic drill powered by a SCUBA tank. Mean
lesion size (6S.D.) after lesion infliction was 2.260.3 cm 2 for P. astreoides and 2.060.6
cm 2 for S. michelinii. Recovery of lesions was assessed after approximately 4, 7, 15, 21,
29, 36 and 49 days following lesion infliction and was measured as described by
Meesters and Bak (1993).
   All experimental colonies were collected at the end of the regeneration experiment.
Using a drilling machine with a hollow drill, cores of the skeleton plus tissue were
drilled adjacent to the lesions. The cores had a diameter of 2.5 cm for Porites astreoides
and 1.2 cm for Stephanocoenia michelinii. The live tissue was removed from the cores
and zooxanthellae densities and chlorophyll a and chlorophyll c 2 concentrations were
determined as in Szmant and Gassman (1990).
   Recovery of lesions was expressed as the percentage of the initial lesion area closed.
Regression slopes (percentage lesion area closed versus depth) were calculated at each
time interval using least-squares linear regression (Sokal and Rohlf, 1995). Regression
slopes were compared using the T 9-method and Gabriel’s approximate method (Sokal
and Rohlf, 1995).


3. Results

3.1. Regeneration

   The data show that regeneration is influenced by depth in both coral species (Fig. 1,
2). When lesion recovery is depicted versus time for the 10 shallowest (5–10 m for
Porites astreoides, 5–16 m for Stephanocoenia michelinii) and 10 deepest (24–34 m for
P. astreoides, 21–30 m for S. michelinii) experimental colonies, a consistent pattern is
visible (Fig. 1a,b). During the first 3–4 weeks more tissue was regenerated in lesions on
shallow water colonies than on deep ones, whereas after that time the recovery was more
or less similar.
   In shallow water colonies of both coral species an average of about 40% of the lesion
area was recovered in just 4 days, compared to 20% in the deep water Porites astreoides

and only 5% in the deep water Stephanocoenia michelinii (Fig. 1a,b). In both species,
shallow and deep water colonies had regenerated an average of approximately 90% of

end of the experiment. Considering all colonies along the entire depth range, only 15%
of all lesions in P. astreoides and 10% in S. michelinii were closed after 49 days.
   The regression slopes of the regeneration curves (i.e. percent lesion area recovered vs
depth) were initially negative in Porites astreoides and Stephanocoenia michelinii, but
later approached zero and ultimately became slightly positive (Fig. 2). The largest
difference with depth (i.e. steepest slope) at any time was observed at day 4 in both
species (Fig. 2). The relatively strong negative slope indicates that recovery is much
higher in shallower water colonies than in deep water colonies. The slope in both species
gradually became less steep and as of day 29 in P. astreoides and day 21 in S. michelinii
it became zero (Fig. 2), indicating there was no difference any longer in the lesion
recovery between shallow water and deep water colonies. Afterwards, the regression
slopes became slightly positive in both species, indicating that recovery was slightly
higher in deep water colonies compared to the shallow ones (Fig. 2). The slope at day 4
was significantly lower than the slope at day 36 in P. astreoides, while in S. michelinii
the slope at day 4 was significantly lower than the slope at day 21, 30, 37 and 49 (Fig.
2).
   The pattern of regeneration versus depth differed somewhat between Porites as-
treoides and Stephanocoenia michelinii. At days 4, 7 and 15, the regression slope was
steeper in S. michelinii than in P. astreoides (Fig. 2), indicating a larger difference in
recovery between shallow and deep water colonies in S. michelinii during the first 15
days of the regeneration process.

3.2. Zooxanthellae

  Densities of zooxanthellae did not differ significantly with depth (P . 0.209) in
Porites astreoides or in Stephanocoenia michelinii (Fig. 3).

3.3. Chlorophyll

   The total chlorophyll concentration (i.e. chl. a 1 c 2 ) per algal cell did not show any
significant differences with depth (P . 0.090) in Stephanocoenia michelinii or in Porites
astreoides (Fig. 4). For the chlorophyll a /c 2 ratio a significant increase with depth
(P 5 0.028) was found in P. astreoides (Fig. 5), although only a relatively small part of
the relation could be explained by the factor depth (r 2 5 0.12). In S. michelinii, the
chlorophyll a /c 2 ratio did not show a significant variation with depth (P 5 0.510).


4. Discussion

   The data in the present study show that regeneration varies with depth in both coral
species. The recovery of artificial lesions in Porites astreoides and Stephanocoenia
michelinii is negatively related with water depth, but only during the initial phase of the
regeneration process. When the lesions are almost closed, however, the mean percentage
of recovered lesion area is similar for shallow and deep water colonies. Bak (1983)
studied the recovery of lesions in P. astreoides colonies at 10 and 23 m depth and found

   Colonies of Porites astreoides and Stephanocoenia michelinii did not show signs of
photoacclimatization with depth by means of an increase of the total chlorophyll
concentration per cell or a variation in the zooxanthellae density. It is known that various
coral species are able to photoacclimatize when living at greater depths (Chalker et al.,
1988). As a response to the reduced light levels deep water corals often have increased
levels of pigments in their tissue (Porter et al., 1984; Battey and Porter, 1988). A further
indication of lack of photoacclimatization in P. astreoides and S. michelinii is the lack of
a decrease in the chlorophyll a /c 2 ratio with depth. Chlorophyll c 2 is much more
efficient in absorbing the blue light available at greater depths. A decrease in the
chlorophyll a /c 2 ratio with depth would therefore indicate a relative increase in the
chlorophyll c 2 concentration compared to that of chlorophyll a, allowing for a more
efficient use of the light available at depth (Battey and Porter, 1988).
   The lack of chlorophyll acclimatization in combination with reduced light levels at
increasing depth may be the responsible factors which could explain the faster response
of tissue recovery in shallow water corals compared to deep water corals in Porites
astreoides and Stephanocoenia michelinii. Regeneration of tissue requires, apart from
nitrogen acquired through heterotrophic feeding, a considerable amount of energy which
must be supplied by the polyps surrounding and in the vicinity of the lesion (Meesters et
al., 1994, 1997a; Oren et al., 1997). In shallow water, corals are often metabolically
supersaturated with light resulting in a reserve of excess energy (Muscatine et al., 1984;
Spencer Davies, 1984; Edmunds and Spencer Davies, 1986), while lipid production has
also been shown to be light-dependent (Patton et al., 1977; Crossland et al., 1980; Battey
and Porter, 1988). Consequently, deep water corals receiving lower light levels may be
restricted in their energy reserves. Porter et al. (1984) calculated that shade-acclimatized
                 I. Nagelkerken et al. / J. Exp. Mar. Biol. Ecol. 234 (1999) 29 – 39    37


colonies of Stylophora pistillata acquire less than half of their metabolic carbon through
photosynthesis, while Stimson (1987) showed that corals kept at experimentally low
light levels had reduced lipid levels. Hence, one possibility which may explain the faster
response of tissue recovery in shallow water corals may be the presence of more
extensive energy reserves, in comparison to deep water corals. Further work on the
carbon and nitrogen budgets of P. astreoides and S. michelinii should indicate whether
this hypothesis is correct. In Stephanocoenia michelinii, larger energy reserves in
shallow water corals are probably not acquired by means of an increase in fatty acids, as
these have been found to be relatively constant along a depth gradient of 3–24 m
(Meyers et al., 1978). An alternative hypothesis is that the deep water colonies harbor
different species of zooxanthellae (Rowan and Knowlton, 1995; Rowan et al., 1997)
than the shallow water colonies, and have a different rate of photosynthesis or
translocation of photosynthetic energy to the coral.
   The fact that ultimately the degree of lesion area which has been recovered is similar
in shallow water and deep water colonies (Fig. 1), shows that regeneration is an
important process in coral ecology (Bak, 1983; Meesters et al., 1994). In spite of the fact
that deep water colonies live at lower light levels and did not show signs of
photoacclimatization, shallow as well as deep water colonies of Porites astreoides and
Stephanocoenia michelinii had both regenerated an average lesion area of about 90%
after 49 days. As the recovery was faster in shallow water colonies during the initial
phase of lesion recovery, it is apparent that the regeneration rate must have been higher
in the deep water colonies during the last phase of the recovery in order to ultimately
result in the same degree of tissue recovery.
   Two of the possibilities which may explain the origin of the energy required for faster
recovery in deep water corals during the last phase of the regeneration process are: (1)
deep water colonies of P. astreoides and S. michelinii may be more dependent on
additional sources of energy through heterotrophic feeding. Various studies have already
suggested that deep water colonies of various coral species, including P. astreoides, are
dependent on allochthonous sources of carbon (Meyers, 1979; Wellington, 1982;
Muscatine et al., 1989). (2) Deep water colonies of P. astreoides and S. michelinii may
be more dependent on translocation of energy for regeneration from other physiological
processes. Energy can be translocated to the regeneration site to the detriment of other
physiological processes. For example, coral growth is negatively affected in regenerating
corals, even in water as shallow as 1–2 m (Bak, 1983) and 7 m (Meesters et al., 1994),
while the fecundity and number of gonads per polyp are reduced near regenerating
lesions of corals at a depth of 11–16 m (Van Veghel and Bak, 1994).

19   ABSTRACT
20   Coral resilience is increasingly recognized as the product of complex interactions between corals, their
21   endosymbiotic algae, and external symbionts such as fishes and invertebrates. While previous studies
22   have documented how coral-associated fishes influence coral growth and thermal tolerance, their role in
23   supporting tissue repair and regeneration following physical injury remains poorly understood. Here, we
24   experimentally test whether coral-associated fishes facilitate wound healing in the coral Pocillopora spp.
25   We imposed small and large wounds and monitored healing in the presence and absence of resident
26   damselfishes. Corals exposed to fishes exhibited faster wound closure and higher photosynthetic
27   efficiency in recently regenerated tissue, particularly for larger wounds. These findings suggest that coral-
28   associated fishes promote the energetic and microenvironmental conditions necessary for tissue repair.
29   Our results extend the emerging understanding of coral holobiont resilience, highlighting the importance
30   of external symbionts in sustaining coral recovery from physical disturbances.
31
32   Keywords: Coral regeneration, Mutualism, Damselfish, Pocillopora, Wound healing, Coral resilience,
33   Dascyllus flavicaudus

34   INTRODUCTION
35
36   Coral reefs are increasingly understood as complex symbiotic systems not only defined by the well-
37   known partnership between corals and their algal symbionts, but also shaped by a broader network of
38   external associations with fishes and invertebrates. Many of these coral-associated fishes and
39   invertebrates live in an intimate relationship with corals, influencing the physical and chemical
40   environment directly surrounding coral tissues(1). These external partners have been shown to affect
41   nutrient cycling(2), predator-prey dynamics(3), sediment removal(4,5), parasite loads(6), and water flow(7),
42   collectively affecting coral physiology, growth, and survival(2,8,9). As research on these external symbioses
43   grows, it is becoming clear that the resilience of reef-building corals may depend as much on these
44   ecological partnerships as on their internal symbioses(as reviewed in 10).
45
46   Despite this growing appreciation, much of the focus to date has centered on how these associations
47   influence coral responses to large-scale, chronic stressors such as thermal bleaching and ocean
48   acidification(2,11,12). However, corals also routinely face localized, physical injury from predation, storms,
49   disease, and human activity(13). The ability to heal tissue following injury is a fundamental component of
50   coral resilience: e.g., if left unhealed, a wound can be colonized by macroalgae or pathogens, potentially
51   threatening the survival of the entire colony(14-16). However, wound healing and tissue regeneration can
52   impose substantial energetic costs that compete with other metabolic demands: damaged corals exhibit
53   reduced growth rates, compromised reproductive output, and depleted lipid reserves, particularly in
54   branching species like Pocillopora(17,18). While we know that intrinsic factors such as wound size,
55   energetic state, and algal symbiont density influence healing rates(13), far less is known about how the
56   surrounding biotic environment may shape these repair processes. In particular, the potential role of coral-
57   associated fishes to facilitate or constrain wound healing remains virtually unexplored(but see 19 for a notable
58   exception)
               .
59
60   Multiple mechanisms suggest that coral-associated fishes may contribute to wound repair. By excreting
61   ammonium-rich waste, these fishes provide nitrogen that can be readily assimilated by algal symbionts (2,9),
62   potentially enhancing photosynthetic productivity and fueling the energetically demanding process of
63   tissue regeneration. Their territorial behaviors also reduce exposure to corallivores and other physical
64   disturbances that could disrupt wound closure(20,21). Furthermore, their constant movement within coral
65   branches enhances localized water flow and oxygenation, especially at night when respiration can drive
66   hypoxia inside branching coral colonies(7, 22,23). This additional oxygen delivery could help sustain the high
67   metabolic demands of wound healing under conditions that would otherwise limit tissue repair. These


2
 68   distinct pathways suggest that coral-associated fishes could modify the physical and energetic
 69   microenvironment in ways that promote regeneration, yet their role in this process has not been
 70   empirically tested.
 71
 72   Here, we directly test whether coral-associated fishes facilitate wound healing in their hosts.We
 73   hypothesized that coral-associated damselfish enhance both the rate of wound healing and the
 74   physiological recovery of host corals. Using colonies of Pocillopora spp., we inflicted small and large
 75   wounds and monitored healing trajectories in the presence and absence of resident damselfishes. This
 76   experiment provides a novel test of how external symbiotic partners contribute to coral regeneration, and
 77   more broadly, illuminates the functional importance of coral-associated biodiversity in promoting
 78   resilience to physical disturbance.

 81   METHODS

 82   Study Site and System

 83   We conducted this experiment in August 2024 at the Richard B. Gump South Pacific Research Station
 84   (17°30′S, 149°50′W) on the northern shore of Mo’orea, French Polynesia. The site features a mosaic of
 85   shallow patch reefs consisting of, among others, the branching coral Pocillopora. Species-level
 86   identification of Pocillopora could not be visually or genetically resolved, and is so referred to by genus.
 87   Patch reefs are interspersed with sand and coral rubble. Pocillopora colonies provide structural habitat for
 88   a range of coral-associated species, including trapeziid crabs and damselfishes, which can influence coral
 89   physiology and resilience(1,2,12,21). Among this community of exosymbionts is the yellowtail damselfish
 90   (Dascyllus flavicaudus), which is one of the most abundant fishes that occupy Pocillopora. We therefore
 91   quantified the effect of D. flavicaudus on wound regeneration and photosynthetic efficiency in
 92   Pocillopora spp.

 93   Specimen Collection and Experimental Setup

 94   We collected 72 coral fragments from mature Pocillopora spp. colonies (>20 cm diameter) without
 95   damselfish from a reef in the lagoon on the north shore. From the same reef, we also collected 18 adult
 96   Dascyllus flavicaudus. We transported corals and fish separately in seawater-filled coolers to the
 97   laboratory, where we mounted each coral fragment on a ceramic frag plug (Oceans Wonders) using
 98   marine epoxy (Z-Spar, Kopcoat, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). We randomly distributed 12 fragments across
 99   each of six 95 L flow-through aquaria supplied with unfiltered seawater at a rate of ~6 L h⁻¹. We held fish
100   separate from corals during the acclimation period.

101   Following a 3-day acclimation under ambient light and temperature conditions, we assigned 4 fragments
102   in each tank to one of three treatments: control (no wound), small wound (avg: 1.48 ± 0.398 cm2), or large
103   wound (avg: 3.82 ± 1.08 cm²). We created wounds on the upper surface of fragments using an airbrush
104   and a circular stencil to remove surface tissue while standardizing wound size and location. Immediately
105   after wounding, we randomly introduced six D. flavicaudus (total biomass ~36 g) into half of the tanks (n
106   = 3). Tanks without fish served as controls.

107   We fed all tanks—regardless of fish presence—2 g of TetraMarine Saltwater Flakes (Tetra US,
108   Blacksburg, VA, USA) three times per day (morning, midday, and evening). We removed uneaten food
109   after 10 minutes via a siphon. We scrubbed tanks and coral plugs daily to prevent algal overgrowth.

110   Monitoring Wound Healing, Growth, and Photosynthetic Performance


  3
111   To assess wound healing and tissue regeneration, we photographed all corals with an Olympus TG-6
112   digital camera and ruler scale bar immediately post wounding. We then randomly selected one fragment
113   from each wounding treatment per tank for daily photography (Figure 1). On day 21, we photographed all
114   wounds again. Using the initial images, we measured the initial wound size (cm2) using ImageJ. Using the
115   final images, we quantified the wound area that remained unhealed, which we determined by the absence
116   of visible tissue, regardless of polyp formation. We then calculated the healing rate (cm2/day) as the
117   change in wound size (initial-final) divided by the duration of the experiment: 21 days.

118   To assess coral growth, we measured the buoyant weight of each coral at the start (day 0) and end (day
119   21) of the experiment(24). At the end of the experiment we also measured the surface area of each fragment
120   by wax dipping(25). For each wounded fragment, initial wound size was subtracted from total surface area
121   measurements to provide an accurate measure of the initial surface area of live coral. We calculated coral
122   growth (mg/cm2/day) as the area-adjusted calcification rate: (final buoyant weight - initial buoyant
123   weight)/((surface area - initial wound area) * 21).

124   To assess coral physiological status on day 21, we measured dark-adapted photosynthetic efficiency
125   (Fv/Fm) using pulse-amplitude modulated fluorometry (Diving-PAM, Walz, Germany)(26). For corals
126   without wounds, measurements were taken near the upper face of the fragment in the same region where
127   we made wounds in the other corals; for wounded corals, measurements were taken near the center of the
128   wound. Throughout the healing process, turf algae colonized open wounds. By day 21, algae persisted on
129   the wounds of eight corals. We therefore carefully removed the algae with tweezers before taking the
130   PAM measurements. Of these eight corals, seven were in tanks without fish and six had large wounds.
131   We compared the corals with algae to those without algae, and since Fv/Fm did not differ significantly
132   between these groups, (p = 0.106), the results we present below do not distinguish between the corals that
133   were vs. were not colonized by algae.

134   Statistical Analyses

135   We performed all statistical analyses in R (v4.3.1). To assess wound healing rate (cm2/day), we fit linear
136   mixed-effects models —with wound healing rate as the response variable—using the lme4 package. Fixed
137   effects included fish presence (present or absent), wound size (small, large), and their interaction, with
138   tank ID included as a random effect to account for non-independence.

139    To analyze fish and wound effects on coral skeletal growth, we used an ANCOVA-style approach where
140   we included log(Initial Mass) as a covariate in a full model with treatment effects. This approach
141   simultaneously estimates the allometric coefficient (b) while testing for treatment effects, avoiding the
142   bias that would occur if b were estimated from pooled data ignoring treatments. We fit a linear mixed-
143   effects model with log(Final Mass) as the response variable: log(Final Mass) = α + b × log(Initial Mass) +
144   Fish + Wound + Fish × Wound + Tank where Fish (present or absent) and Wound (none, small, large)
145   were fixed effects, and Tank was included as a random intercept to account for non-independence of
146   corals within the same tank. We then used maximum likelihood estimation and tested the significance of
147   treatment effects using likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models. The estimated allometric
148   coefficient was b = 0.990 (95% CI: 0.986, 0.994), indicating near-isometric growth. For visualization, we
149   calculated size-corrected growth using the b-value estimated from the full model: Scaled Growth =
150   log(Final Mass / Initial Mass^b) We also calculated absolute growth rate as mass change per unit surface
151   area per day: Growth Rate (mg cm⁻² day⁻¹) = (Final Mass − Initial Mass) / ( Total Surface Area - Wound
152   Area) / 21 days. This metric represents the mass of calcium carbonate deposited per cm² of coral tissue
153   per day.

154   To evaluate variation in photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm), we used linear mixed-effects models with the
155   same fixed and random effects structure. For all models we assumed normal error distributions and


  4
156   homoscedastic variances and evaluated those assumptions using diagnostic residual plots. To assess the
157   significance of fixed effects and their interactions, we used likelihood ratio tests via the anova() function
158   from the car package. We report chi-squared statistics and associated p-values for all terms.

159   RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

160   The effect of coral-associated damselfishes on wound healing depended on wound size (fish × wound
161   interaction: χ² = 4.416, p = 0.036, Figure 2). Fish presence increased healing rates for both wound sizes,
162   but the effect was more pronounced for large wounds. For small wounds, fish increased healing rates by
163   60%, from 0.048 cm²/day (95% CI: 0.027–0.069) without fish to 0.079 cm²/day (95% CI: 0.065–0.092)
164   with fish. For large wounds, fish increased healing rates by 64%, from 0.131 cm²/day (95% CI: 0.094–
165   0.168) without fish to 0.204 cm²/day (95% CI: 0.169–0.239) with fish. Although the proportional increase
166   was similar, the absolute effect of fish was 2.4 times greater for large wounds (0.073 cm²/day) than for
167   small wounds (0.030 cm²/day). Additionally, turf algae frequently colonized wounds during the healing
168   process. By day 21, algae persisted on 8 corals and was biased towards tanks without fish (7/8) and with
169   large wounds (6/8).

170   The effect of damselfish presence on coral photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm) depended on wound type
171   (fish × wound interaction: χ²₂ = 10.898, p = 0.0043, Figure 3A). In the presence of damselfish,
172   photosynthetic efficiency was similar across all three wounding treatments: No Wound = 0.686 (95% CI:
173   0.679–0.693), Small = 0.674 (95% CI: 0.657–0.691), and Large = 0.684 (95% CI: 0.677–0.692). In the
174   absence of fish, however, photosynthetic efficiency declined with increasing wound size: No Wound =
175   0.684 (95% CI: 0.676–0.691), Small = 0.657 (95% CI: 0.629–0.685), and Large = 0.633 (95% CI: 0.610–
176   0.656). Fish presence had minimal effect on unwounded corals (+0.002 Fv/Fm units) but progressively
177   greater effects on wounded corals: +0.017 for small wounds and +0.051 for large wounds. Thus,
178   damselfish presence positively influenced photosynthetic efficiency, and these effects were most
179   pronounced when wounds were larger and metabolic demands associated with wound repair were
180   presumably higher.

181   In contrast to effects on healing rate and photosynthetic efficiency, and contrary to previous studies (9, 2),
182   we observed no significant effects of fish presence, wound size, or their interaction on coral growth after
183   controlling for initial coral size (Fish: χ² = 0.001, p = 0.978; Wound: χ² = 1.783, p = 0.410; Fish ×
184   Wound: χ² = 5.224, p = 0.073; Figure 3B). Given prior studies demonstrating positive effects of fish on
185   coral growth, and our finding that fish increased photosynthetic efficiency and healing rate, we suggest
186   that that the absence of detectable calcification responses may reflect the short experimental duration of
187   our study. It may also reflect insufficient sensitivity of the calcification metric or the lack of an effect of
188   acute wounding stress on calcification, but this seems unlikely given past studies(2, 9, 27).

189   These findings highlight the critical contribution of coral-associated fishes to coral resilience, specifically
190   their roles in tissue regeneration and physiological recovery following acute injuries. Although the exact
191   mechanisms remain to be elucidated, nutrient subsidies from damselfish excretion likely enhanced
192   symbiotic algal productivity, providing the additional energy required for wound repair (2, 9). Additionally,
193   turf algae persistence in wounds was markedly more common in corals without fish, suggesting that fish
194   may also facilitate wound repair by mitigating the energetic constraints of competitive exclusion.
195   Consistent with this interpretation, corals without fish also exhibited depressed photosynthetic efficiency;
196   however, this may reflect impaired symbiont performance or reduced tissue availability for symbiont
197   colonization, rather than a direct effect on symbiont physiology alone.

198   In addition to nutrient benefits, damselfish behavior, such as constant swimming within coral branches,
199   may improve water flow and oxygenation; factors that are particularly important during the nighttime
200   period when metabolic demands of wound healing are high and hypoxia risk increases(7, 22, 23). Together,


  5
201   the nutrient and behavioral benefits of coral-associated fishes likely enhance the coral’s capacity to
202   regenerate tissue efficiently.

203   Our results contribute to the emerging perspective that coral-associated fishes significantly enhance coral
204   holobiont resilience, by adding the facilitation of tissue regeneration to the previously established roles of
205   fishes in promoting coral growth and increasing thermal tolerance(21, 28). By demonstrating that
206   damselfishes actively facilitate coral regeneration following injury, we underscore the functional
207   importance of external symbiotic relationships in buffering corals from both chronic stressors and acute
208   physical disturbances. Preserving these ecological partnerships will likely be crucial for sustaining coral
209   regenerative capacity and enhancing long-term reef resilience.

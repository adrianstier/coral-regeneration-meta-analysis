Abstract. Three mechanisms aiding recovery and expansion of a population of Arropora palmala on a
patch reef that was directly in the path of Hurricane Andrew were documented: rapid wound healing,
high rates of asexual recruitment, and rapid growth rate of the new recruits. In addition, the growth
pattern of new recruits was assessed in order to quantify the initial sequence of StrUCtUrdl changes
during the transition from recruit to adult morphology. Wound healing was initially rapid (1.59cm of
linear growth per month). but slowed with time. Nine months after the storm, 72% of 218 hurricane-
generated fragments of A . pulmarci had cemented to the bottom, becoming new asexual recruits. Within
18 months of the storm, the number of fragments in the same area had increased to 271, average
fragment size was larger, and 94% had become recruits. Recruits showed complex patterns of branch
formation, including rapid growth of solitary proto-branches, differential growth of proto-branches
dependent on recruit orientation, and dominance within aggregates of proto-branches. We present a
conceptual model that suggests A . pulmata is adapted to disturbances of both low intensity and high
frequency (conditions typical of reef flat zones) and episodic high intensity and low frequency events
(hurricanes and tropical storms).

35-60 YOhave been measured, with a strong relationship between survivorship and
fragment size (HIGHSMITH et a/., 1980; ROGERSet a/. 1982). In addition, BAK&
CRIENS   (1981) found that stabilization of fragments was dependent on substrate
type; there was a significant relationship between substrate type and both fragment
survivorship and fusion. However, in only a few studies was the upward growth
rate of new branches forming on hurricane-damaged colonies measured (ROGERS
ct a/., 1982; GLADFELTER,    1991).
   For A . palnwta recruits, quickly establishing upward growth leading to the adult
morphology is very important as the arboreal shape may confer an advantage in
competition for light (SHINN.1972; PORTER,1976); without this, A . pdmata is an
inferior direct competitor for space (LANG,1973; JACKSON, 1991). However, the
early structural changes that take place during the transition from a relatively flat
fragment to the complex tree-like structure typical of an adult A . palniutci are
largely unknown.
   The objective of this study was to determine initial recovery rates and processes
for the storm-damaged population of A . palmuta on Elkhorn reef. Three
approaches were used. First, we measured the pattern and rate of wound-healing
on colonies that were still standing after the storm. Second, temporal changes in
the size-frequency distributions of fragments and new recruits were measured in
order to quantify rates of sexual recruitment. Last, the pattern of initial growth of
new recruits was assessed in order to quantify the initial sequence of structural
changes during the transition from recruit (flat) to adult (tree-like) morphology.
This was accomplished by sequential measures of the abundance, height, and
spatial distribution of newly forming branches. We tested the hypotheses that the
orientation and surface area of new recruits would significantly affect rates of early
growth and survivorship.

           4. Distribution, abundance, and growth rates of proto-branches

In spring and early summer of 1993. many fragments had begun to regenerate. The process was initiated
after most of the fragments had reattached to the benthos by accreting calcium carbonate skeleton
between the fragment and substratum (LIRMAN        & FONG,1996a). By mid-June of 1993. small proto-
branches appeared on the top and sides of nearly all of the surviving fragments.
   On I July 1993 we niarked 6 fragments. noted orientation of each fragment as a deviation from the
upright pre-hurricane position, and measured the initial size both photographically and irt s i r i r as longest
length by widest width. We mapped the distribution of proto-branches on each recruit and measured
their maximum height. These measurements were repeated on 6 October 1993. Analysis of variance
was used to determine if there were any significant differences between the average height of proto-
branches among fragments for each date.
   Two hypotheses were developed to investigate differences in distribution and growth of proto-
branches among colonies. The first hypothesis was that there would be differences in number and height
of proto-branches on recruits due to the degree of change in orientation that the fragment experienced
as a result of transport during the hurricane. The underlying rationale is that more energy would have
to be expended to initiate upward growth by a coral colony that was further from the original
orientation. and growth would thus be slower on fragments with greater deviations. Marked and
measured fragments were divided into 2 categories. One group was rotated 180 from their original
orientation on the ‘mother’ colony; these fragments were standing on what were the branch tips of the
original colony. The rest of the fragments were lying flat on the benthos. or shifted 90 from the original
orientation. Differences in proto-branch height due to fragment orientation were tested with /-tests.

abundance and/or height of proto-branches: more energy should be available lo allocate branch growth
if more polyps were present on a recruit. We used regression analysis to quantify the relationship

Between the October and December sampling periods, the amount of disturbance
to A . pulniutu increased considerably due to strong winter wind events (NWS,
1993; LIRMAN     & FONG,1996b). Three of the five colonies marked in October for
our lesion study were completely shattered, with large pieces still bearing tags
transported to new locations. For those A . pulmutu colonies that survived this
secondary disturbance (two colonies with 6 identifiable lesions), the rate at which
tissue regenerated and expanded linearly over the exposed skeleton slowed with
time (Table 1). The healing rate was faster in the first period (October-December)
than in the second (December-March). There was no relationship between the
initial area injured and the mean linear distance the ‘healing lip’ of the coral tissue
moved over each injury (r’ = 0.006; P > 0.05); thus, the rate that the ‘healing lip’
advanced over a wound was not dependent on the initial size of the wound.
Variability in the rate that the tissue moved over the injuries was great; both the
highest and lowest rates were measured in the first time period. This variability
occurred because wounds did not always heal evenly, with one edge of tissue
moving further than others.

December this algal community changed to turfs dominated by red filamentous
algae. These algal turfs did not vary visually among injury sites. Except for lack of
a crustose coralline component, the turf did not appear to differ from the ubiquitous
turf community that occupies hard substrates on other parts of Elkhorn reef
(LIRMAN,    unpublished data). We did not observe colonization by sponges or coral
recruits.

   One interesting observation about the growth patterns of the new recruits was
the similarity to patterns of growth in higher plants, especially trees. In a similar
analogy, TUNNICLIPFE     ( 1983) compared regeneration and growth of Acroporu L'CJI'-

the growth of new recruits of Acroporapulmutu and trees. First, the proto-branches

suggesting a seasonal or temporal cue. Alternatively, growth of recruits may be
synchronized by the need for a recovery period before they can produce new
growth. In addition, since a considerable amount of energy may be spent in the
stabilization process, upward growth may be delayed until this is achieved. The
second similarity to tree growth is that, within an aggregate, one of the proto-
branches 'dominates', and the others have reduced growth rates. This is analogous
to plants exhibiting apical dominance. In trees, this dominant-subdominant
relationship is tightly regulated by growth hormones; the controlling factors in
new A. palniatu recruits are unknown. We hypothesize that, after sufficient growth,
'solitary' proto-branches may become 'aggregated' at a larger scale, and this pattern
of one becoming dominant and subsuming the rest may be repeated until the
Elkhorn colony has the typical adult morphology.
   Several authors believe it unlikely that organisms can evolve adaptive responses
to disturbances such as hurricanes, because they occur very seldom relative to
generation times (PAINE,     1979; SOUSA,  1984). However, hurricanes may not occur
infrequently for corals that have continuous growth and often reproduce by colony
fragmentation. These traits may result in high generation overlap and the long-
term persistence o f a given genotype (POTTS.1983). Thus, a given population may
be exposed to several major disturbances during its lifespan. VOGL(1980) maintains
that organisms and the communities in which they exist must be considered in
terms of the species' lifespans and life cycles.

seasonal storms, groundings) occur at regular intervals. In the case of A . pulmutcr
populations, the smaller-scale disturbances they encounter on a seasonal basis may
have resulted in the evolution of features that may also aid recovery after major
disturbance events such as infrequent hurricanes.

Initial recovery of a population of the branching coral Acroporupulmaru damaged
during Hurricane Andrew (Biscayne National Park, northern Florida Reef Tract,
USA) was documented. Recovery was measured in three ways: the rate of wound-
healing of damaged adults, hurricane-generated asexual recruitment rate, and the
pattern and rate of initial upward growth of new recruits. Immediately after the
storm, wounds on surviving adult colonies - formed when branches were
broken ~-healed rapidly ( I .59 cm of linear growth per month); however, this
